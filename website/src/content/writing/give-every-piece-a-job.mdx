A year of content analysis keeps teaching me the same lesson from different directions: the biggest gains in editorial work no longer come from publishing more. They come from managing what you publish as a portfolio — fewer pieces, each with a defined job, connected into arguments that outlast any single piece.

That sounds like strategy-deck language, so let me make it concrete. A portfolio approach changes four specific decisions.

---

## 1. Every piece gets a role before it gets a brief

Most content is commissioned with a topic and a deadline, and judged later by whichever metric is nearest. That is backwards. The role comes first, because the role determines everything else — length, depth, distribution, and above all how success is measured.

Four roles cover most editorial work:

- **Reach** — built to find the largest relevant audience. Judged on audience, not conversion.
- **Conversion** — built to turn intent into action: a sign-up, a download, a completed exercise. Judged on completion, not traffic.
- **Practical utility** — built to be *used*: guides, checklists, how-tos. Often modest traffic, strong engagement, long shelf life.
- **Narrative development** — built to advance an argument the organisation is making over time. Sometimes low reach with clear intent; its job is to exist, be citable and connect.

The test of role clarity is simple: can you say, before publishing, which single measure this piece will be judged by? If the answer is "all of them," the piece has no role — it has a hope.

The most expensive failure this prevents is misdiagnosis. A narrative-development piece judged on traffic looks like a failure and gets defunded. A conversion asset judged alongside articles looks like a superstar and warps the benchmark for everything else. Neither number is wrong; both comparisons are.

## 2. Reach is earned by construction, not by topic

Looking across the highest-reach work I've been close to, the same three construction choices repeat — and none of them is "pick a trending topic":

1. **A clearly named audience.** Not "businesses" — sole traders facing a tax deadline, practice owners choosing software, finance leads at manufacturers. The narrower the address, the stronger the pull.
2. **A real decision or deadline.** Content anchored to something the reader must actually do — a regulatory date, a renewal, a purchase — borrows urgency it never has to manufacture.
3. **A concrete next step.** The reader finishes able to do something, not just aware of something.

Deadline-led utility for a named audience, with a next step. It is almost embarrassingly simple, and it consistently beats cleverer, broader, better-written pieces aimed at everyone about nothing in particular.

## 3. Event content becomes thesis, not recap

Events are where portfolios quietly leak value. The default output — a recap of what was said — has no audience beyond the people who were there and no life beyond the following week.

The alternative is to treat the event as evidence collection. Go in with a question the wider narrative needs answered; come out with an interpretation the audience couldn't get by attending. The strongest event piece is not "what happened at the conference" but "what the conversations at the conference reveal about the problem we've been arguing about all year." A recap serves the event. A thesis serves the argument — and keeps earning after the event is forgotten.

## 4. Volume stops being the lever

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of most content operations: the plan grows every year, the team doesn't, and the fix is always to produce faster. AI has made producing faster genuinely easy, which has quietly broken volume as a strategy — if everyone can publish more, publishing more is worth less.

The portfolio response is to reallocate rather than add. Fewer, higher-conviction flagship pieces built with the reach construction above. Stronger internal linking so connected pieces behave as one argument. Deliberate promotion of the pieces that are quietly working — every portfolio I've examined has under-promoted assets outperforming their distribution. And a maintenance budget: refreshing a proven piece is often the highest-return editorial work available, and almost no plan allocates for it.

None of this reduces ambition. It relocates it — from the length of the publishing list to the strength of the system behind it.

---

## The portfolio question

If you run an editorial operation, the four decisions compress into one planning question:

> For each piece on next quarter's plan — what is its job, who exactly is it for, what argument does it extend, and what would make it worth refreshing a year from now?

A piece with good answers belongs in the portfolio. A piece without them is volume — and volume is no longer the game.
