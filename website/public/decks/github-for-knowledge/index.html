    Why every company needs a GitHub for knowledge — not just code

    Software teams solved organisational memory twenty years ago. Everyone else still runs their most valuable knowledge through attachments and leaving drinks.

    • Asavin Wattanajantra
    • 7 August 2026
    • 20 slides

    The opening

    Ask a software team where their code lives

    A one-word answer.

    Ask a marketing team where their knowledge lives

    A pause. Then a list.

    Some of it’s in the research folder, some in decks, some in a doc from the launch, quite a lot in Sarah’s head. Sarah left in March.

    Neither team is smarter than the other.

    One of them solved organisational memory as an engineering problem twenty years ago. The other is still treating it as a filing problem.

    That difference used to be survivable.

    AI has made it expensive — because for the first time, the return on well-structured knowledge isn’t limited by how many humans have time to read it.

    01

    What version control actually solved

    Storage was never the hard part. Any shared drive stores things.

    What software teams actually got

    One source of truth
    Not “final_v3_FINAL_amended”. A single canonical version, with disagreement handled through review rather than parallel copies.
    History survives
    What changed, when, and — because every change carries a message — why. A decision made in 2019 is still inspectable in 2026.
    Change is reviewed
    Improvement is a process, not an accident of whoever last had the file open.
    Everything is connected
    Not ten thousand unrelated files, but a system, where you can trace what depends on what.

    None of these guarantees has anything to do with programming.

    The asymmetry

    When knowledge rots in a codebase

    The build breaks.

    When knowledge rots in a marketing team

    Nothing breaks.

    The team just quietly re-purchases research it already owns, re-learns lessons it already paid for, and re-argues positions it already settled.

    02

    The knowledge everyone else loses

    Look at what a typical B2B organisation actually knows — and where that knowledge physically sits.

    Assets the organisation paid for

    ~ where it currently lives

    • −customer research → a PDF attached to an email thread
    • −interview transcripts → never kept
    • −the campaign argument → a deck, minus the reasoning, which was verbal
    • −tone, claims, positions → the heads of two senior people
    • −refined AI prompts → personal chat histories

    Almost none of it is in a state where the organisation could find it, trust it, build on it, or hand it to an AI system.

    Rich in knowledge, bankrupt in memory.

    Compounding isn’t a mindset. It requires somewhere for the compounding to physically happen.

    03

    Running the experiment on myself

    I moved my own methodology into a repository and started treating professional knowledge the way engineers treat code. Not metaphorically — literally.

    What that looks like in practice

    frameworks/
    Versioned documents, so a framework has a history rather than a latest copy.
    evidence/
    Research lives alongside the arguments it supports, so a claim can be traced to its source.
    workflows/
    Editorial workflows written down as workflows, not carried as habits.
    prompts/
    A library, where a prompt that worked gets kept, improved and reused instead of dying in a chat window.
    commit history
    The part I didn’t anticipate: a development log of the thinking itself, showing how a framework evolved and what changed my mind.

    The published layer is open: github.com/asavinwa/editorial-intelligence-os

    Effect one

    Structuring the knowledge improved the knowledge.

    Writing a workflow down exposes the steps you were skipping. Versioning a framework forces you to notice when you’re contradicting last month’s version.

    The repository isn’t a record of the methodology. Maintaining it turned out to be the methodology running.

    Effect two

    AI became dramatically more useful. The models never changed.

    An agent pointed at a structured, versioned, interconnected body of knowledge can find the relevant evidence, respect the established positions, reuse the proven workflows and draft inside the house argument. The same agent pointed at a shared drive full of orphaned PDFs can only generate plausible text.

    The difference isn’t the AI. It’s what the AI has to stand on.

    04

    Documentation just became a competitive advantage

    Which is why this argument has a deadline on it.

    The multiplier

    Before — a cost centre with a moral glow

    Ten per cent better, for readers who never had time.

    Now — two competitors buy the same models

    One hands them five years of connected research. One hands them a brand deck.

    They will not get the same output, and the gap will not be ten per cent.

    Your own AI tools and your market are grading the same thing.

    AI systems increasingly form their view of what your company is credible about from the evidence you’ve made available — and they reward exactly what version control enforces internally: consistency, connection, and claims that trace to sources.

    To be clear about what this isn’t

    Don’t put the marketing team on GitHub on Monday.

    Git’s learning curve is real, and the platform was built for people who think in branches. The specific tool matters far less than the guarantees it enforces. What you cannot do is get those guarantees from a shared drive, a wiki nobody gardens, or the memory of whoever has been there longest.

    The honest test · one minute

    Pick the most expensive piece of knowledge your organisation produced last year — the research programme, the strategy work, the customer evidence. Could a new joiner:

    • Find it?
    • Understand why it says what it says?
    • See what’s been built on it since?
    • Hand it to an AI system in a usable form?

    If the answer is no, the knowledge isn’t an asset yet. It’s an anecdote with a budget line.

    Engineers stopped accepting that trade a generation ago. For everyone else, the tools have finally made the same refusal affordable — and the companies that notice first will spend the next few years building on top of what they know, while their competitors keep starting again.
